7B. Technical In-Depth: Functional Anatomy of the Regulator

The regulator is the isochronous heart of the mechanical watch. It ensures the rigorous slicing of time through regular oscillations. Mastering its sub-assemblies (connections, guides, and impulse) is essential to understanding chronometry.

📷 WORKSHOP TECHNICAL TABLE — Functional anatomy of the regulator and balance-spring assembly.

Detailed description of the key components of the balance-spring assembly:

  • 1° The Stud: The fixed anchorage peg that secures the outer end of the hairspring on the balance bridge.
  • 2° The Collet (Roller): Small central metal ring crimped onto the balance staff, securing the inner end of the hairspring.
  • 3° The Pivot: The extremely fine and polished end of the balance staff rotating within jewel bearings.
  • 4° The Hairspring: Elastic spring providing the permanent returning torque.
  • 5° The Balance Rim: The peripheral circular ring whose mass determines the moment of inertia.
  • 6° The Balance Arms: Rigid connecting spokes linking the hub to the rim.
  • 7° The Regulator Index: Mobile regulating organ modifying the active length of the hairspring.
  • 8° The Roller Table: Supporting the safety notch and impulse pin.
  • 9° The Impulse Pin (Ellipse): Ruby cylinder receiving and transmitting the impulse between the lever and balance.

7C. Balance Inertia and the Secret of the "Tick-Tock"

1. Understanding Inertia at the Workshop Bench

When handling a balance wheel, one immediately perceives its resistance to changing speed: this is inertia. For a watchmaker, inertia (I = m · R²) means that the mass of the balance must not only be heavy, but above all distributed as far as possible from the center (on the rim). A balance equipped with good inertia acts like a stabilizing flywheel: it is less vulnerable to wrist micro-shocks or gear train jolts.

2. Anatomy of a Beat: Where Does the "Tick-Tock" Come From?

This characteristic sound, listened to on the timing machine, is not a single noise, but the sum of two ultra-fast mechanical events generated at each vibration/alternation:

1. THE "TICK" (Entry impact)
Locking of the wheel on the entry pallet.
2. THE "TOCK" (Exit impact)
Symmetric locking on the exit pallet.

* The "Tick" (First impact): Occurs when the escape wheel tooth rests and locks onto the entry pallet of the lever.
* The "Tock" (Second impact): Corresponds to the symmetric locking on the exit pallet on the other side of the cycle.
Between these two sounds, the impulse pin crosses the fork in absolute silence. Listening to the tick-tock means listening to the "breathing" of the watch: a regular rhythm confirms proper banking/beat setting on the bench!

3. The Harmony of Oscillation: The Formula Demystified

The period of a balance wheel (T = 2π · √(I / C)) is simply understood like a scale: I represents weight and its distribution (balance inertia), while C represents spring force (hairspring stiffness). To adjust the watch, the watchmaker either acts on the regulator index (to modify the active length of the hairspring C) or on the balance screws/weights (to modify inertia I).

8A. Frequency and Chronometry: Understanding Hertz, Vibrations and Their Stakes

In watchmaking terminology, we constantly juggle between Vibrations per hour (vph), Hertz (Hz), and the number of audible beats. Let's clarify to dispel any confusion:

  • The Hertz (Hz): Represents a complete oscillation (round trip) per second. 1 Hz = 1 cycle/second.
  • The Vibration / Semi-oscillation: Each forward or backward movement of the balance releases an escape impulse. Since a complete cycle includes a forward and a backward movement, 1 Hz produces exactly 2 vibrations (a "tick" and a "tock").
  • Concrete example: A standard modern movement oscillating at 4 Hz performs 4 cycles per second, i.e., 8 vibrations per second (4 × 2 = 8), yielding 28,800 vibrations per hour (8 × 3600 sec).

Advantages and Disadvantages: Choosing the Frequency

The choice of a caliber's frequency is never a matter of chance; it results from a sharp technical compromise between chronometric stability, power reserve, and mechanical wear:

📉 Low Frequencies (e.g., 18,000 vph / 2.5 Hz)
Advantages: Less friction, reduced wear on wheel trains, and excellent power reserve (the gear train consumes less energy). Visually, the slow oscillation of the large balance wheel is a magnificent spectacle in traditional Haute Horlogerie.
Disadvantages: More sensitive to shocks and wrist movements; a micro-shock disturbs the watch's rate more heavily.
📈 High Frequencies (e.g., 36,000 vph / 5 Hz)
Advantages: Exceptional chronometric stability. Thanks to high speed and high inertia, the balance recovers its equilibrium position blindingly fast after a disturbance. Essential for measuring short fractions of time (high-precision chronographs).
Disadvantages: Increased energy consumption (requires powerful mainsprings), heavily stressed lubrication, and accelerated mechanical wear without modern materials (silicon, surface treatments).
