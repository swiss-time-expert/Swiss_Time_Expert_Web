2. Motor Organ (Energy): The Barrel & Winding Systems

The barrel is the central component for storing potential energy in a mechanical movement. The assembly consists of four fundamental elements:

  • The Drum: Cylindrical box housing the spring and bearing the outer teeth that mesh with the center pinion.
  • The Mainspring: Elastic blade wound inside, whose outer end is fixed to the inner wall of the drum.
  • The Barrel Arbor: Pivoting axis between the bridge and mainplate, equipped with a hook for the inner attachment of the mainspring.
  • The Cover: Closing element ensuring axial guidance of the arbor and protecting the mechanism from dust.

4.1 Generalities and Role of the Energy Accumulator

In a mechanical movement architecture, initial energy is supplied by the user winding the crown. The system acts as an accumulator converting kinetic energy into elastic potential energy.

The classical watchmaking functional chain breaks down into five major subsystems:

  • Energy Source: Manual or automatic winding.
  • Accumulator: Barrel and mainspring.
  • Counting & Transmission: Going train.
  • Distribution: Escapement.
  • Regulation & Display: Regulator and time display.

4.2 The Barrel and its Mainspring

4.2.1 Technical Description of the Assembly

The barrel assembly typically consists of four fundamental parts:

  • Barrel Drum: Cylindrical box housing the spring and serving as the arbor pivot. A notch on the inner diameter secures the outer spring end to drive the drum.
  • Mainspring: Elastic blade wound inside the drum.
  • Barrel Arbor: Pivoting axis between the bridge and mainplate, featuring a hook to attach the inner end of the spring.
  • Barrel Cover: Closure element that also aids in guiding and pivoting the arbor.

4.2.2 Physical Principle and Geometric Evolution

The spring is a rectangular blade. By attempting to return to its initial form after being tightly coiled around the arbor hub, it produces the torque needed to power the movement.

Historically, the mainspring geometry evolved from a classic spiral wind to an S-shaped (reverse curve) bridle, optimized for the material's yield strength to ensure more linear energy delivery. The drum's peripheral teeth transmit this torque directly to the train.

4.3 Kinematic Analysis: Number of Turns and Spring States

To model spring behavior, three fundamental states of development are defined:

  • N'' : Number of turns when the spring is fully wound (tightly coiled around the arbor).
  • N' : Number of turns when uncoiled inside the drum (resting against internal wall R).
  • N₀ : Theoretical number of turns in free state (uncoiled outside the barrel).

The difference between the arbor hub space (r) and internal drum radius (R) is calculated to reserve ~50% occupied surface and ~50% free surface, maximizing energy storage capacity.

Fundamental Development Formulas:

  • Total development turns (ΔN_t):
    ΔN_t = N'' - N₀
  • Useful turns in the barrel (ΔN_u):
    ΔN_u = N'' - N'

4.4 Design Parameters and Engineering Formulas

4.4.1 Theoretical Spring Length

The active length L of the spring depends on drum and hub geometry:

L ≈ (R² - r²) / (2 · e)

(Empirical workshop note: to optimize real energy, this theoretical length is generally increased by 10% to 33% depending on movement dimensions).

4.4.2 Maximum Torque Moment (M_opmax)

When the spring is fully wound around the hub, maximum torque is calculated as:

M_opmax = (E · h · e³) / (12 · L) · 2π · ΔN_t

Where:

  • E = Modulus of elasticity of the material (2.2 × 10⁵ to 2.3 × 10⁵ MPa or N·mm⁻²).
  • h = Height (width) of the spring blade [mm].
  • e = Blade thickness [mm].
  • L = Spring length [mm].
  • Δ N_t = Total development turns.

4.4.3 Material Properties

Modern high-horology mainsprings use structural precipitation-hardening alloys based on cobalt, nickel, and chrome (e.g., Nivaflex). These antimagnetic materials offer high ductility, corrosion resistance, and withstand heavy elastic stress without permanent plastic deformation.

Allowable stress limit (σ_max): Between 2840 MPa and 3340 MPa depending on grade.

Constant Force: The Fusee-Chain System

This historical mechanism acts like a continuously variable transmission gearbox to compensate for mainspring torque loss as it unwinds.

  • Max Winding: Spring is strong; the chain pulls on the small diameter of the fusee (short lever arm).
  • Unwinding: Spring weakens; the chain pulls on the large diameter of the fusee (long lever arm), perfectly equalizing the force transmitted to the escapement.
